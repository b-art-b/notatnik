The lighthouse at Cape Varden had been dark for eleven years before anyone thought to ask who had switched it off. The question came, as most questions in the village did, from a child, and it was the sort of question that grown men found it easier to answer with a shrug than with the truth.

Marta Ilves, the keeper's granddaughter, climbed the hundred and forty steps on a windy Tuesday in March with a flask of tea, a torch that did not work, and the stubborn conviction that something had been left behind. At the top she found the lamp room swept clean, the brass polished, and a single chair facing the sea.

Nobody in the harbour could explain the polish. The council had cut the lighthouse budget in the spring of 2015, the last keeper had retired to his sister's house inland, and the keys had gone into a drawer at the town hall. Yet the glass was spotless, and on the sill lay a logbook whose last entry was dated that very morning.

The entry was short. It recorded the wind, the visibility, and the passing of a grey freighter at dawn, in the neat upright hand that the village remembered from the old keeper's notices about lost dogs. It ended with the words: all quiet, lamp ready, nobody asked.

What followed was a month of quiet detective work, most of it carried out over kitchen tables. The retired keeper swore he had not been near the place since the day he left. The fishermen, who had every reason to want the light back, swore the same. The only fingerprints in the lamp room belonged to the girl who had found it, and to a person nobody could name.

The answer, when it arrived, was disappointingly ordinary and therefore entirely convincing. A woman from the next bay, widowed that winter and sleepless, had taken to walking the cliff path at night. She had found the door unlatched one evening, gone in out of the rain, and stayed because the work of polishing brass was the only thing that made the hours pass.

She had never lit the lamp, she explained, because it was not hers to light. She had only kept it ready. The village, which had spent eleven years pretending not to see the dark headland, discovered that it had been watched over all along, and did not know whether to be grateful or ashamed.

The council reversed its decision in May. The lamp was lit on the first of June, with a small ceremony and a large amount of cake, and the logbook now has two hands in it: the old upright one, and a rounder one that signs each entry with a single initial and the words, nobody asked.
